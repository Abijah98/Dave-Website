Talking down — how the educated Left gatekeep the moral highground

by Sebastian Day · 13 min read

Let’s start simple. You’re talking down to them. Ever wonder why it is you just can’t seem to get through to anyone? It’s because no one in their right mind tunes into feral criticisms from a pseudo-intellectual college educated brat, or some pompous ass like in Good Will Hunting, who’s only aim is to appear smarter. It doesn’t matter if what you’re saying is right and needs to be heard. It doesn’t even matter if you don’t think I’m talking about you (I am), because you’re not a brat, you are an intellectual, and you’re certainly not feral (probably)—that’s how they see you, or anyone on the Left at this point. Just brainwashed liberals who’ve been taught to hate men, and laws, and the nuclear family, who have weaponized empathy in order to hide from the tough choices of reality because you have too many feelings.

If anything you’re doing your cause and anyone you claim to advocate for a huge disservice when you refuse to gain a modicum of perspective outside of the exhausting ecochamber of mutual masturbation that is your social circle. Think about it, how badly do you have to verbally abuse someone that the only way for them to hold a scrap of self respect is to decide you’re crazy and to side with a humanity-hating pedophilic oligarch? There’s something wrong here, and it’s not that they’re all evil (use your brain, that isn’t possible), they’re just ill-informed by their circles and upbringings, and the majority of individuals who could liberate them from that (you) are demanding the conversation is spoken in a language THEY DON’T SPEAK.

Honestly though, while it is your job to fix this, it isn’t entirely your fault. Despite working your knuckles bloody trying not to succumb to certain biases, there’s one that gets harder to shake the more informed you become. In 1989, Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber coined the term ‘The Curse of Knowledge’, in their economic article, The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings: An Experimental Analysis [1]. Here they state, “The curse of knowledge makes personal expertise seem more widely shared than it is, making it difficult for people to convey their expertise to others and reducing the apparent need (from the perspective of the better-informed individual) for such a transfer of knowledge.”

One of the tests they ran was an exercise between students where they gave a portion of them secret information that the other students remained unaware of. These informed students were then asked to predict the actions of their fellow unknowing students based on the general info given to all. The findings showed that the informed students struggled to predict how their classmates would act. In super layman terms, they kept forgetting their fellow students didn’t know what they did, and so it wouldn’t affect their actions like it would if they did. They couldn’t check their own biases when assessing how someone else thinks, “Better-informed agents do not fully appreciate the informational advantage they possess.”

The curse of knowledge was proven again in Elizabeth Newton’s doctoral dissertation she wrote at Stanford University in 1990, The rocky road from actions to intentions [2]. Within her dissertation lay an experiment called ‘Tappers and Listeners’ known now as the Stamford Tapping Experiment. The premise for this experiment was simple; test the curse of knowledge by providing twenty-five well known and simple songs to a group of people who would then tap the beat for a separate group who had no prior knowledge of what songs would be played. The tappers (those who knew the songs) were asked beforehand to guess the percentage of how many songs the listeners could identify. They guessed the listeners would manage to identify the right song 50% of the time. They were wrong. The listeners only managed to guess the song correctly 2.5% of the time. The problem was those who knew the songs had the melody in their heads when they were tapping. As Dr Newton states in her dissertation, “The tappers had the knowledge of the song in their heads, 'heard' it as they tapped, and attributed that [same auditory experience to the listener].”

Much like the subjects in the test, college educated left-leaning activists fail to remind themselves that the people they’re arguing with often do not have the same information or access to topic-relevant nomenclature. Instead, what most do is retort data that, yes, is totally relevant, but never explain why it is without a brand new retort of inaccessible mumbo.

So much of what people say these days fall under buzzwords and jargon, which applies to both the Left and Right of politics. Even individuals who describe themselves as apolitical have their own triggering or solidary phrases. But as we see from the example above, this is far from just a political issue except that it also extends to it. The term, ‘curse of knowledge’ came from economists, and can be spread through just about every topic. Have you ever tried to ask a corporate financier about their day-to-day schedule? Do we all understand what a KPI is? I sure as hell don’t understand half the words my father tells me about his mountain climbing hobby. I don’t know what a whipper is, or what he means by ‘smearing’, but I know that if I actually decided to learn he’d teach me on my level, not his. And, no, it’s not necessarily important that I do know a whole lot about climbing lingo, or inner office jargon, but some subjects hold more weight than others.

Let’s take the medical field. We all know how it feels to have a doctor speak to us like we, too, studied eight years for a medical degree. Remember in the movie 50/50 when Joseph Gordon-Levitt’s character is diagnosed with schwannoma neurofibrosarcoma? A lack of transparency between doctor and patient is incredibly frustrating, even frightening, which is why John P Lalor, David A Levy, Harmon S Jordan, Wen Hu, Jenni Kim Smirnova, and Hong Yu created the study published as Evaluating Expert-Layperson Agreement in Identifying Jargon Terms in Electronic Health Record Notes: Observational Study [3]. In the study, researchers wanted to evaluate the gap of knowledge between doctors and patients. They did this by testing if doctors and medical illiterates (normal people) could identify what words were included in medical jargon, and while of course the doctors scored better on jargon identification (25.6% for laypeople and 59% for doctors) the test really wasn’t about who knew better but how large the gap of knowledge was. The results showed that laypeople encountered terms they thought they understood but didn’t, and that despite doctors having more knowledge on the subject, a staggering 41% of the words they came across they assumed patients would know. Now, the question is, who do you think is responsible for bridging this gap? Would you look at that tiny 25.6% from regular people and simply tell them to educate themselves (as many patients and patient relatives have had to do to navigate medical spaces)? If so, and this is inflammatory, perhaps your true priority is less about educating and more to be recognised as knowledgeable. If that’s the case, cool—there’s a certain prestige that comes from being recognised by ‘your people’, but then perhaps you aren’t the best candidate to be debating anyone in opposition. Not if the goal is to change their mind.

But of course it can’t be as simple as fixing the way you talk, because while it’s a start, the problem runs deeper than exclusionary language. A whole other paradigm of how we operate towards individuals of polarity has to shift—how we view one another in the first place. We seem to think that anyone who disagrees with us is a moron, and anyone who knows less than us is not worth conversing with. That they should shut up and listen to you tell them what’s what.

This philosophy belongs to something known as the deficit model, the notion that the problem of misinformation can simply be fixed with the spreading of true information by experts who understand it. That public opinion will change for the better so long as members of the scientific (or otherwise educated) community just lay out the facts. But the problem is, why would they listen? Truly. Many people seem to think that just because you know you have access to knowledge with credible evidence, that automatically means that everyone else knows you have access to knowledge with credible evidence, and that you are a credible source because of it. This line of thinking just doesn’t work. Mostly because if they didn’t know the knowledge in the first place, they’re not going to know how important the sources that you’re providing are. And they’re not going to see your knowledge as useful, in a similar way that you don’t see their knowledge as useful.

The deficit model has been used for the past century, seemingly first being coined in the 1930s, and it can be split into at least two relevant fields—science communication, and education. The reason this matters is because both fields use it differently, and for that reason when talking about it as just one thing, the information can sound contradictory.

Let’s start with the deficit model in science communication. According to Oxford Academic [4], “In essence, the model asserts an epistemic priority for science and scientific information: that scientific knowledge should be paramount in making decisions on science-related issues, that this knowledge should be communicated from scientists to audiences who do not have this knowledge, that scientists should be in control of this communication flow, that the nonscientific audiences receiving this knowledge will be grateful for this, and that they will make better decisions as a result.” Essentially, it assumes the space in laypeople’s minds where knowledge should be is just empty space waiting to be filled by the intellectual authority. It completely ignores the possibility that preconceived understandings could already occupy that space (sometimes inserted by the last guard of intellectuals who thought they were right). On top of that, it ignores the potential for mistrust among lay people, aka non experts, towards scientific findings or academia. Such mistrust can come from anywhere—religion, tradition, even class disparity, as high education fields have mingled with and long served the interests of the higher economic classes. The scientific deficit model completely ignores that.

On the other hand, the educational deficit focuses a little too much on this issue, and in the totally wrong way. While scientists state the public should be grateful of the knowledge they bestow upon them, educators explain the reason they’re not getting through to some learners is simply because their background (specifically—cultural, religious, racial, familial) does not allow them to. Oxford Reference [5] states it is, “A perspective which attributes failures such as lack of achievement, learning, or success in gaining employment to a personal lack of effort or deficiency in the individual, rather than to failures or limitations of the education and training system or to prevalent socio‐economic trends.” Oxford Reference itself also references the 1981 White Paper: A New Training Initiative: A programme for action [6], which is a regulatory document written under Margeret Thatcher’s Tory premiership to restructure the United Kingdom’s educational and employment systems (for context, Britain has been stupid since). This piece of legislation has come under heavy criticism over the years, most in part for some of its apparent intentions (lowering unemployment stats nationwide by placing the unemployed in a training course that didn’t fall statistically under unemployment, and funneling women and minorities through the program as a means of keeping them in medial roles), and the outcome of the training programs themselves (overall poor and inconsistent training, an incentive for employers to hire cheap labour youth over unionized adult workers, and a low job retention outcome for most youth in the program). In the white paper, it states, “It is applying these extra resources to help secure longer term reforms in the quality of training and bring about a change in the attitudes of young people to the value of training and acceptance of relatively lower wages for trainees.” Even before the initiative was in effect, the attitudes of these individuals was always assumed to be a primary issue. When we see that the entire initiative was designed on the back of the deficit model, placing the blame on the individual for their predicament and not the system, it’s easy to see why it didn’t help them.

This model has been challenged and discredited by many experts in just about every academic field at this point. One recent example is the research study by Hatsue Koizumi and Hiromi Yamashita, Deficit Lay or Deficit Expert: How Do 'Experts' in Environmental Projects Perceive Lay People and Lay Knowledge? [7]—published in SAGE Open in 2021. It presented the perception experts held of lay people and the knowledge they possessed. The activities tested involved research and planning projects along with information sharing, and by the end, many of the candidates recognized the limitations of their own scientific models. While the deficit model places the blame on lay people for having no understanding, the ‘Deficit Expert’ tries to find gaps in their own knowledge that data may not present as a means to build bridges of understanding before attempting to update public understanding. The deficit expert also recognized lived experience as valid, shifting the perspective as a situational truth rather than an outlying anecdote not reflected in data and therefore dismissable. “The results have shown experts perceive not only that lay knowledge is somehow valuable but also that an expert can be “deficient” in terms of their knowledge and their competence to understand reality,”—this means that for experts who understand this, the goal changes from simple ‘higher’ information transfer to self-reflection, where they can keep an open mind to what lay knowledge may actually provide further insight to context. In the conclusion, researchers state, “It raises doubts about a traditional, clear-cut explanation of the dichotomy between experts and lay people. On the contrary, part of the findings reveal variation in the communication mode used when disseminating science, not to educate but to respond to demands from lay people to learn more about their topic. We are not saying that lay people have the correct view or would not benefit from more scientific knowledge but that experts should listen carefully to lay knowledge, including what is behind that knowledge because it might be something very important to be included in any environmental decision. As environmental decisions are often connected to politics, ignoring certain people or certain positions may cause tension and conflict.”

At the end of the day, it’s hard not to see those on the other side of discourse as opposition, and it can be somewhat gratifying to see that opposition as inferior. But opposition is a term too close to enemy, and sadly once it comes to that the talking stage is over. That can, and does happen, but if we aim to change minds we cannot lose that special ability everyone has to connect with one another and empathize with the lived experiences which inform all of our world views. Even when individuals come from the same background, that defining moment that forwarded your mind to growth happened exclusively to you and your personal context, and not to them. There is no such thing as coming from the same place, you ≠ them. Knowing this, we can attempt to see the deficit in ourselves, and perhaps allow that to foster humility (even in our correctness), which in turn fosters a more meaningful exchange.

I come from a missionary family. My parents moved to Southeast Asia before I was born, and regardless of how you feel about that, they understood something about sharing ideas that are different from those they’re sharing to. You do it in their language, not the other way round. You don’t assume that simply due to their cultural differences they’ll never understand your message, you connect with their culture and shed biases of your own. Politicians like James Talarico who is a Democrat has gained notable appreciation with everyday Republicans in the state of Texas by doing just this. Several times during his appearance on YouTube channel Jubilee [8], Talarico speaks to Texans using traditionally conservative language. He uses terms like “pull yourself by your bootstraps” and “well-fare queens” to talk about corporations instead of the lowerclass, ultimately framing what may have had pushback into something non-Democrats could consider. Now, does that mean he’ll succeed in his up-coming election for the senate? Not at all—but if not, it won’t be because he failed to treat ordinary uninformed (even misguided) individuals with dignity.

Maybe there will come a time where words don’t change anything, and opposition must be met with action. But for as long as we believe in discourse over physical acts of violence, we must try our hardest not to wrap what is considered ideology for the people in language not used by half of them. This is not a one and done task. Even though I am aware of the bias the curse of knowledge brings I, too, fall for it all the time. As stated at the start of this essay, the more knowledge we accumulate the stronger the bias becomes. The goal is not to erase the bias once and for all; it’s to understand that it’s there, we have it, and we must assess it within us every day.

The Scamperings of Tony Spot

by Sebastian Day · 28 min read

His curious nose shuffled the autumn leaves into bunches. Trees overhead were not bare yet, but that would soon change as it did every year. The floor of the woods was always fullest on the cusp of the season. Foliage yet to shrivel sharing with the first of the fall. It’s never busier, with woodland animals foraging for their last morsels. But Tony Spot wasn’t looking for food. He knew he didn’t have to. No, this was a waggish pursuit, enjoying the privilege of aimless curiosity.

On the outskirts of Chickpool Village, outskirting St. Ives, the place was simply called the farmwoods. Because just past the stonebridged creek, sat Downborough farm. As is with the rurals, Downborough wasn’t the only local farm; not even the largest of the farms surrounding Chickpool but it was the one closest to the woods. Also home for Tony Spot, a curious boy who loved more than anything to get his nose in places we’d not care to look.

It was in these cracks and crevasses, under leaves and rocks, between buttress roots, where he found the most interesting stories. Following his nose as it unfolded each scent’s tale. Through the brackens and berryless brambles, under birdsong choir, the distant slosh of stream water rushing, and scurrying squirrels in trees, Tony Spot followed. Past the muddy patch, which was always muddier than the rest of the dirt. Sometimes he took a detour, risked losing the scent, just so he could wallow for a moment in its cooling filth. He always jumped in the creek afterwards to wash up, but even then little Sally Downborough would bathe him once he came home.

Today however, Tony Spot did not muddy himself. The scent he had was way too interesting to leave, even for a minute. There was strangeness to it that didn’t go unnoticed by either him or his sniffer. Something wildly musky in an otherwise domestic countryside. Even the squirrels would come down for nuts. But not this…no, this would creep and hide. This would growl and pounce, and for Tony Spot, there was nothing more exciting.

The farmwoods, themselves, were perfectly mapped in Tony Spot's head. From Downborough stonebridge, and out the other side into Chickpool, there was the path. This was what he knew first, since a pup. Little Sally would take him on a leash through the woods and into town, where villagers gave him pats. The path mostly went in a straight line through the woods, besides the occasional bend where a tree stood in the way. Or the one with the big rock where he sometimes saw children climb. It was his favorite bend, because of that.

There was badgerden, on the far end of the stream. He knew the least about this place on account of the badger. Not the friendliest, and liked to be left alone. He knew badgerden was especially thick with bushes and leaves, and soft enough dirt to burrow. But that was all he knew. That was where the cliffhangers led. Shame; there’s a certain peace in closure we all seem to share, bipedal or nah. Something about the end of the pattern. The close of the back cover, where you once again glimpse at the blurb, but with different eyes. But, again, the badger was mean, and there would always be a new story to wash away any distaste from the last one.

On the right side of the path, coming from the bridge, was the pond. Newts always dashed beneath the wet surface when Tony Spot came sniffing. The frogs did not. They’d stare at him with their bright yellow eyes, and their voidfully black pupils that showed no life behind them. But from the speed of the throat flutters, Tony Spot could see their emotions clear as day. Each tempo conveying every thought they had. None of them ever moved when he approached. Sometimes they croaked but he didn’t speak frog. The water was still and smelly, and Tony Spot liked to dip his toes in it. One time he went in too deep and his foot became tangled in the reeds. He was lucky Farmer Downborough was there to get him out, and he had been more careful since. Neither Farmer, nor Sally, were with him this particular day.

Through the trees ahead from the pond were the chirpy woods. The trees grew thicker, so more birds nested in their branches. Tony Spot knew he was there when the chirps became louder and pine cones scattered across the floor. There was never much of a story there. Just the endless birdsong and maybe the rummaging of squirrels through the pine cones. He never went there unless Sally took him. She found staring up into the trees more interesting than he did. All the best stories were on the ground, where she never looked, no matter how many times he barked.

Tony Spot would never enter the village humanless. Because he was a good boy. He kept to the farmwoods, and hoped he’d find closure for this odorous tale there. Lucky for him, the smell was getting stronger, as he found himself deep into the ferns and brambles (close to the muddy patch) he called the undergrowth crawlspace. Vines wrapped, twisted and tugged as the thorns clawed and pulled. No one standing over this sea of shrubs could possibly know what moved beneath. In the deepest parts, the leaves never even met the ground. There were no gaps between ferns and thorns for them to fall. Instead, they rest dead on the top of the bushes and covered the crawlspace even better. In the winter, even the snow had trouble reaching the bottom.

Many dogs avoided the undergrowth crawlspace, scared of thorns and getting trapped. Tony Spot was not one of them. Too much lay undiscovered. Too much relied on its mystery. To hide. It was the same for his story. His nose forced the musk up his nostrils. It would be unbearable to walk away now. For us, it would be like only sipping from a full glass when parched. Or turning off your favourite song seconds before crescendo. Down in this crawlspace, it grew stronger. It grew feral.

Tony Spot was not afraid. To be truly curious, you have to side-step fear. To enjoy it, you have to cast fear out entirely. Besides, other than the reeds in the pond and the badger in badgerden, what had he to be afraid of? Maybe whatever was at the end of the scent would ultimately scare him too, and that would be okay—because then, he would know what it smelled like. As he got to the thickest part of the crawlspace, he heard something shuffling ahead. Rustling ahead. This would be no cliffhanger. Not today. His eyes focused but still saw nothing but vines and little branches. He winded through them and when he couldn’t do that, he squeezed. The rustling grew louder, but still he saw no movement. Whatever it was understood the crawlspace enough not to get caught in the thorns, or perhaps moved better than he did. From what his nose told him, these were the only possibilities. It was too large a creature to move around unaffected.

A flash of orange.

What was that?

Brighter than a dead leaf, and those don’t tend to move that fast…or yip.

An odd sound. One he’d never heard before.

Soon enough Tony Spot came face to face with its maker, the source of the smell. The end of his story, or perhaps just the start. As he went nose-first through the final curtain of bracken leaves, he saw it—staring at him, and he froze.

A wall of thorns stood between them, but both could see the other just fine. Tony Spot wondered how this creature had managed to get on the other side of that fence, the branches tied across one another so thickly. But it was this, our little friend was sure, that was the reason it did not flee. Both animals knew Tony Spot could not pass the thorns. Its fur was bright orange, like the flash he’d seen before. It’s blinkless eyes, a murky amber. It looked like him…almost like him, but Tony Spot had met other dogs. He knew many looked different than him. Various sizes, a different colour coat, larger or smaller ears, a longer snout, or barely even one. But their smell was always the same. And this was different.

Tony Spot felt wary. The uncanny valley he stood between, staring at the creature, unnerved him. It was frozen in place, its eyes intensified in their colour as the black circles came smaller. Tony Spot sniffed the ground around him and the thorns on his side of the wall. It reeked in such a good way, but he smelled something under the creature’s musk.

Female, was what his first sniffs at this closeness could tell. Fully mature. Mud, berries, all outside smells. No residual man-stink that all pets, including himself had. He smelled fear. A ferocious fear, and under that, an intention.

He darted his head back just in time to see the closed mouth of the snap, and the doggish animal growled after. It was Tony Spot who was now frozen, unsure of what to do next. Should he say something? What could he say? Other than…

“What are you?”

“You don’t know?” she asked, with a feline smoothness he’d never heard from anyone like him. Oh, this was different. He was certain of it now. But just before Tony Spot could get the answers his nose craved, he heard…

“Tohhhhhhhnieeeeeeee! Tony! Where are youuuuuu!”

Both animal’s ears perked. It was Sally’s voice. So loud for her size, and louder still for them. They looked at each other once again, and he saw the animal snarl.

“You want to hurt me,” Tony Spot said, a startled look in his eye.

“It is you who’s hunting me. Why else would you come through this labyrinth of thorns to find me? They sent you!”

Who were they, Tony Spot wondered? Did she mean Sally? Or Farmer Downborough? What would either of them want with such an unfriendly creature?

“Tohhhhhniiiieeee!” Sally called again.

“That means home time,” Tony Spot said, “goodbye.”

“What—,” the creature started, her ears appearing to relax a little, but Tony Spot was gone. Through the bushes as quickly as he’d appeared. No barking, no calls, just gone. Home, he’d said, and home he went, scampering out of the underbrush and splashing past the mud again. Darting to the path where Sally waited for him.

“Oh, Tony! You’re all muddy again!”

&

Food was always good on the farm. Aside from dry Kibble, Tony Spot was usually given leftovers. Somehow Mrs. Farmer Downborough always cooked too much, but Tony Spot never complained. He hoped she would always make that mistake, remembering the stale half cup of Tesco-brand dog food they gave him at the shelter where little Sally chose him. He’d ran to her, leaping into her arms, taking in the first whiff of Capri Sun and Weetos that stayed permanently clung to her.

That was the first time he’d heard her shrill little giggle, which he loved from the start. Obviously, Tony Spot never assumed she wasn’t there for him. Just him; and that she’d gone to choose one of many. But the moment she caught him, he’d caught her. “I want him! Only him!,” and since, Sally’s voice meant more to Tony Spot than the air in his lungs.

Tony Spot had just turned two, yet had managed to keep all of his puppy charm that won the Downboroughs over in the first place. A farm is a wonderful place for a dog with endless energy, and curiosity. So many smells and so much space to run. Even if Farmer Downborough had enough of his playfulness, he could always go someplace else. Like the woods, or the sheep field. It felt as if he were insanely lucky, but apparently it happened all the time. The people at the shelter were always talking about the farm their latest dog went off to.

The last of the hunter’s chicken was scoffed, and so was the Kibble beneath it in his stainless steel bowl. Tony Spot felt Sally’s gentle little hand on his head and he flattened his ears so she could pet him. Mrs. Farmer was still sitting at the dining room table while Farmer Downborough did the dishes. Farmer had high blood pressure. His pink skin became pinker whenever he was worked up, and as he washed those dishes, Tony Spot saw he was very pink, indeed.

“The Becketts saw it, and the Dallybrook’s farmhand! Running through one of their fields. One r’two chickens missing from Stan’s, and that’s just two farms over!”

“I’m sure it’s just passing through, deary. Just looking for food,” Mrs. Farmer said as she sipped on her evening PG Tips.

“That’s just it! Food! And where’ll he find it? I’ll tell you if that damn fox gets any’f mine, I’m marching up t’the village council, n’asking for permission t’shoot it.”

“Oh, Daddy, you’d shoot it?” Sally asked.

“Would y’rather it get one of your chickies? I’ve seen you feeding extra t’the speckled one. Be a shame’f some fox came’n swiped’er. They always go for the fat ones, y’know,” Farmer said playfully, but they all knew he meant every word.

Tony Spot slept outside, but it wasn’t at all bad. In fact, he had his own little doggie house, with a stinky pillow he loved to snuggle. In a couple months' time, once it became too cold, they’d move the pillow into the vestibule, and he’d sleep with the shoes. Luckily he wasn’t a chewer. But for the time being, he’d just snuggle his pillow harder as the autumn winds began to whistle.

That night, Tony Spot couldn’t sleep. He was far too stimulated from the day had, with all the new things he’d seen and heard. Could the new thing he’d seen in the farmwoods be the same as the new word out Farmer’s mouth? Fox? Is that what you are?

If so, he began to understand the fear. Farmer’s gun looked no different to him than the other tools Farmer pulled out of the shed. Except this one, he didn’t keep in the shed, and was only taken out once Sally was at school. Farmer Downborough took Tony Spot out one morning and shot a pheasant. The sound those metal tubes had made sent our boy running, and he only calmed down once he saw Farmer’s gentle stroll—bird in hand. Over time, he’d started fetching the pheasants for him, instead of running the other way, and while he was no longer afraid, Tony Spot understood it was because Farmer Downborough never pointed that thing at him. He thought about the creature snarling and snapping, and how she had said: “They sent you.”

&

Since Chickpool was so close, Sally walked to school, and Tony Spot often went with her as she crossed the farmwoods. The morning fog was thick and milky, and Tony Spot could barely see ten feet in front of him. But his nose was as sharp as ever. The crisp morning air was coffee to his sniffer, and it was already telling him which way to go.

Sally stopped by at the end of the woods, and gave Tony Spot a ‘good boy’ pet. He then sat on the edge of the path and watched her go, waiting for that fog to take her as she skipped onto the second stone bridge that led to the village. Then he was off.

From what he could tell, the underbrush crawlspace was empty. But the smell was still there, and it was on everything. The fox had certainly made herself familiar with the area. Every bracken and vine had her musk. Tony Spot caught the tiniest whiff of blood on a thorn. He was not surprised. He had scratched himself plenty when he first explored the crawlspace.

The chirpy woods were already awake with birdsong. He wondered what the fox had been doing here. Almost nothing of interest was found here, unless you like looking up into trees as humans seem to. But the smell was there too, and after finding the tufted remains of a squirrel in the leaves, he understood. The blood was still damp, so he knew it was a fresh kill.

You came here for food, he thought, because there’s no one else to feed you.

Tony Spot realised he could not think as he would. He, who ate leftovers and slept on a pillow, and followed scents for the spirit of adventure. Tony Spot would understand the fox that way. But he still had her scent, and he could follow it.

More fog hovered over the pond than anywhere else, but Tony Spot saw her outline through it. She was drinking. Of course she would be, he thought! After eating, he was always thirsty. Seconds after seeing her, she lifted her head to see him. She probably has a good nose, too, he thought.

She wasn’t running, but Tony Spot thought better than to bounce triumphantly toward her. He remembered the last time he’d come too close. Instead, he moved slowly and curiously toward the pond. His gaze did not stay fixed on her as hers did with him. He looked around, and sometimes went back to sniffing the leaves. Soon though, he was close enough to see her colour.

“You’re alone,” the fox said.

“I’m not hunting you!” Tony Spot ended up saying, somewhat earnestly, as if she would bolt at any moment.

She said nothing.

Tony Spot began to sniff the reeds close to the water, which still sat between them. He then sat down and tilted his head at her. “Are you a fox?” he asked, and she nodded.

“What is your name?” he then asked.

“We do not have names. Because they do not own us.”

“What do you call each other?” Tony Spot asked, and the fox laughed.

“We are not like you. With your constant yapping for company.”

Tony Spot wondered if that’s what he did. Did he yap? Sure, when Mrs. Farmer held his dinner and he couldn't help himself. Or when Sally came home from school. What was so bad about that? Surely better than sleeping in the thorns and finding your own food. And snapping at guests! But Tony Spot did not say this. He was much too interested to growl.

“Why do the humans hate you?” he asked instead, to see if he would hate her too.

“That is always what they do,” the fox said, “they make you and yours know our scent, and you follow it right to us! Then you tear us apart!”

“Why?”

“Does it matter? Is there anything we could do to deserve being ripped to shreds? Not even eaten. Sometimes just left in the leaves.”

Who were these humans Tony, Spot thought? They didn’t sound anything like the ones he’d met. They loved him. Perhaps what she knew as humans were in fact different creatures entirely. Something like the fox to him—similar, but not the same in any way. But Farmer did say he’d shoot her, and then he remembered why.

“You eat their chickens.”

The snarl was almost instant. “You expect us not to eat when we’re hungry? Shall I wither when the woods are bare? The woods they make bare? Better to eat and be hated than ignored and starve.”

Now they stripped the woods, too, Tony Spot thought? What else would she say the humans were guilty of? He had never seen anything of the sort. Then he thought of the rats…and the pheasants. Tony Spot did not notice, but his tail had stopped wagging.

“Maybe you should leave,” Tony Spot said. He realised he didn’t want to hear Farmers' gun go off, and to see him walking back carrying that orange coat. Or worse, make him carry it, like he had started to do with the pheasants. The pheasants were birds. Tony Spot couldn’t speak bird. He ate hunter’s chicken. But it seemed he could speak fox.

“And go where?” the fox asked as she began to prowl along the pondside.

“Where they aren’t,” Tony Spot said, as if that were obvious.

“They are everywhere. Anywhere they aren’t is somewhere they can’t. And anywhere they can’t…I can’t.”

&

After her homework was done, Sally Downborough came out in the evening to have those last minutes of daylight with him. Blue Chuckit! ball launcher in hand, she’d make the ball soar across the field. After, she gave him the heaviest pets. They looked into each other's eyes with matching adoration. Looking at those deep pools of brown, he wondered how such a beautiful creature could possibly hurt anything.

Tony Spot sensed the fox was like the badger, and preferred solitude. Only differing in ability to enforce it with him. The fox was dainty. Sure she was quick and her agility meant he’d never catch her. Although, if he did…well, Tony Spot knew she was not wrong in saying he could tear her apart.

Tony Spot knew he’d never do that. But that didn’t mean other dogs wouldn’t. Oh, there were some bad dogs out there. He would see them dragging their humans along with the leash. Their mouths encased in leather cages…but how would those dogs hurt her like that? They would have to be set free. And who did that, Tony Spot wondered? Were people just like dogs? Were some of them bad, too? What could a bad man make even a good dog do?

Either way, Tony Spot knew the fox could not help being nervous around him. So, while he was not a bad dog, he decided to keep his distance. He still smelled the fox’s musk when walking Sally to school. She was about. Hidden.

Bare trees covered the landscape as fall turned to winter. The number of birds dwindled, as it did every year. The squirrels were easy to see on the leafless branches that stuck out like crooked fingers. They moved with greater urgency than before; needing to collect every last nut they could. Grass was beginning to frost in the morning, and crunched under Tony Spot’s paws.

But one morning, as Sally skipped down the path, Tony Spot realised the musk had faded. In his confusion, he gave a small whine that Sally noticed. “Oh, Tony, what’s the matter?” she asked. He hadn’t realised he’d made it, and so he wagged his tail to put her at ease. She smiled and knelt down. “Oh, you know I’ll be home soon!” she said. He gave her a sniff and she stroked his ear, and they continued down the path to where the trees ended. He knew the drill. She would leave and he would stay, these days glumly, following the path back home. But not this time. He knew that once again, he’d be sticking around. It was the first time a story had come from the absence of a smell.

&

It was after dinner and Sally’s favorite show was on. The living room was always the warmest room in the house. The rug, where Sally and Tony Spot sat, was the traditional Turkish kind that held in all the heat from the radiator. There was a fireplace, but they only used that if it was snowing outside. The heaters were fine for now. On the mantel, were family pictures in frames: one of Mrs & Mr. Farmer Downborough, a handful with Sally when she was younger, and one of her holding Tony Spot. The curtains were still open, but there was no light coming from the windows. Orangey yellow hues glowed out of the lamps scattered across the room on various End and Drum tables. The light swelled warmly while comfortable sleepiness overcame the both of them.

Tony Spot hadn’t found any trace of the fox. Maybe it was for the best, he thought. Why not try to find someplace better? Where no one hates you. Perhaps she was wrong, and there was a place for foxes without people. Or maybe a place where they didn’t feel like shooting them. He thought about what it would be like for him to have to sleep on the cold ground. Thorny bushes as shelter. The search for food that overshadowed that scamper; and even if you were well fed, there would be no time for play. Not with the farmers of the world assuming your business. Was that why the badger was so mean? And why he stayed in his little corner of the woods? The little corner he could call home, so long as he stayed there. But the fox couldn’t stay. She had to move, and keep on moving. No wonder she snapped at him. Fear would make even the goodest boy snarl.

“Time to put ‘im outside,” Farmer said.

The next morning, Sally fed him his cup of Kibble. He knew the good stuff came at dinner, but at least the dry food wasn’t stale. He’d felt the warm air coating the walls of the house as he’d wagged his way to the kitchen. Heaters on in the morning meant they’d accepted it was time. Most likely, Mrs. Farmer would be going for his pillow after her morning cuppa. Sally noticed his tail and let out a grin warmer to Tony Spot than the radiator. “Look! He knows he gets to sleep inside tonight!”

&

End of year stories were always sadder, come winter time being downright tragic. The bird with the broken wing whose final thoughts had been the acceptance that her flock had left her behind. The frozen frog who’d not found a good enough hiding place before the air stayed his limbs, now at the mercy of birds who didn’t mind the cold. The frozen squirrel with the face of self punishment—how could he have forgotten where his nuts were?

Tony Spot was fine with the odd tragedy, so long as he found the final chapter and faced the closure. In fact, he tried harder for those. He felt they deserved it more. Deserved to be remembered, even by the least significant observer. So the story wouldn’t melt with the snow at the start of spring, and disappear into the air as a light breeze. Yes they made him sad, but that’s just what sad stories do.

A kestrel stood, perched on the big rock the children liked. She had her talons sunk into a dead pigeon and was eating. Each peck from her hooked beak came with a flurry of grey feathers. This happened from time to time; a story Tony didn’t have to sniff out. One that landed in front of him. An open book. Beginning, middle and end. All that was left to decide for Tony Spot was what sort of story this was. Certainly the pigeon’s tragedy.

Tony Spot sat quietly and watched as the kestrel ate, waiting for her to finish. It was unusual for him to see one at this time of year. Most birds of prey that would, had migrated already. Lucky for her, pigeons do not—and as she flew off the rock, leaving only bones and tufts of feathers, Tony Spot decided it was a happy story. For whatever reason, she had been late to leave. But now, she would have the fuel to make the journey safely. A happy ending indeed. Maybe that was all the fox needed. Wings.

With each new minute he spent enjoying the grounds he had lost, the more Tony Spot couldn’t help but think that it was the fox’s fault she could not stay. He didn’t want to think it, but he did anyway. Foxes eat chickens; he remembered Farmer Downborough saying so. They came to the farms looking for food, and that was why they were shot. If foxes like her didn’t do that, the farmers wouldn’t need to shoot them. It was as simple as staying away and finding food elsewhere, in woods like these. He wasn’t sure she was right in thinking there were no places like these woods. Why, the Downboroughs had only recently gone caravaning in deeper woodland areas, far larger than the farmwoods. Surely there were foxes there? And he knew if they had seen any, Farmer would not have shot them. He hadn’t even brought his gun. Maybe that was where the fox had left for. Maybe she had heard of it and made the journey there. Tony Spot sure hoped so. She’d learn she was wrong and that there were places for her kind, away from Farmer’s work. Places where food was abundant and that the humans didn’t mind you eating. Places for you, fox.

On his way home he passed the pond again. The poor frogs. Frozen stiff but still alive. Without a hiding spot. Not close enough to the water. Tony Spot never interfered with stories, or how they ended. They were not his stories to change. But this time, he found himself pawing at the ice at the edge of the pond. Pieces broke off; they would freeze over again, now with new little froggies nuzzled to deeper depths where herons would need to work harder for them. They would never know what he’d done, and that was okay. Better even. He had not altered any narrative.

&

There were a few times in the year where paper wrapped boxes tied with glitter ribbons were passed around at the farm house. Most of the time, the colourful parcels were handed to one of the family members while the others sang for them. But the one time it happened in winter, the parcels were for everyone. Tony Spot could not believe the paper. While the Downboroughs fussed around with whatever was inside, all Tony Spot could do was rustle through the rainbow of wrappings until he ran himself to sleep in the warm smother of a fire indoors.

Farmer didn’t go out as much during this time and instead lounged in the big chair next to the couch. The TV was always on, even when nobody was watching. Mrs. Farmer, while still hard at work making too much food for a family of three (and a dog), had far more tea breaks and actually enjoyed a cookie or two. Though they smelled good, Tony Spot was not allowed to have them. He’d tried once as a pup and it had not ended well for him. No matter, there were more leftovers this time of year than any other.

Then a sad thought crept its way into Tony Spot’s floppy ear, and scurried to the forefront of his mind. Even he could not finish all this food. Yet, outside, animals starved. Some were lucky to sleep, Tony Spot knew that, like the hedgehogs that pricked his nose whenever he sniffed them. He never saw those starve. While the badger did not sleep, he was mean enough to withstand the cold. But not everyone was mean like the badger, or slept like the prickly hog. There were some who had to face the cold head on, and hope not to starve. And in this moment, Tony Spot understood better than he ever would, what would lead the fox to take what was not hers, and to risk making an enemy of man. Madness isn’t quite mad when no sense is offered, and to starve when there is food was the most senseless thing one could do.

Tony Spot wondered where she was. He lay on the rug, leaning against the cushioned side of the couch. Had she burrowed in snow? Shivering but alive. His stomach was full. He knew hers would not be. What would lead you to do it? How much hunger to wager death? How could Tony Spot even begin to guess, when he himself had forgotten the feeling.

There had to be some who liked foxes. Would they help her? Like he was once helped? Tony Spot had never seen a fox on a leash. Sally gave him a quick pat on the head as she reached for her glass of juice. A quick reminder for him. That she loved him, and even when sitting up on that couch, eyes glued to the flashing lights on TV, that she had not forgotten him. A love just not meant for some. Was that really how it was? How could that be the case? When love was so warm, even in winter. And swelled out so far that he almost burst each time he felt it. Though he felt it all the time. But it was cold outside.

Later, Sally put on her wellies and winter coat and went outside with Tony Spot. Since he was sleeping inside, they had to let him out to tinkle and Tony Spot could always take the opportunity for one last sniff about. It was with that little sniff, just after his tinkle, that a tensing of muscles turned his frame rigid. And it was not the cold. It was something in the air, quickly moving past but caught in that moment. It was faint, but distinct, and he had smelled it before.

Tony Spot’s ears perked but heard nothing over the howl of frostbit wind, and all he could see in the blackness was white. It had to be her, though he hoped it wasn’t, and that all foxes smelled the same. She would be so hungry.

“Tony?”

He realised, and flopped his ears down, turning to Sally and his pointed tail suddenly curled inwards between his legs. “Tony Spot what’s the matter?” she asked as he trotted past. But Tony Spot did not want to tell her. He made his way to the closed door and waited. After a moment Sally joined him. She found it strange, and wondered if she should tell Farmer. But she didn’t.

The next morning was bright. The sun was alone in the clear sky, and its rays bounced over the sleeping white blanket covering every surface. The stream still ran, though Tony Spot would not dare to run through it, not while it ran that cold.

The smell had faded, but hadn’t left entirely, and Tony Spot couldn’t help wondering what that meant. Had the snow smothered its pungency, or had last night’s wind blown it from a far away place? He needed to know. Early, as soon as they’d opened the front door, Tony Spot went to the farmwoods—without Sally. Once there, he expected the scent to grow as it had the first time he’d smelled it. But it didn’t; even the crawlspace was clear.

He did not stay longer than he needed. Although Sally would probably be having a late holiday breakfast with some hot chocolate, Tony Spot knew it was only a matter of time before she’d come to the woods. Children often came to the farmwoods more in winter. They’d run under bare trees with pale branches, bark lightened by the cold, and throw snowballs. But even if no one was coming today, Tony Spot knew she would come looking for him. It was the first time he’d ever hid from her, but he did not want to know what would happen if she met the fox. He did not want to know if the fox was right.

Tony Spot crossed the bridge, only seeing his footprints. He then realised that if the fox had been around, she would have left her prints, too. For once, Tony Spot would have to rely on his sight over his nose. His eyes were still good, but nothing compared to even an older sniffer. But how hard could it be? Catching orange in the snowscape? She would stick out, even to him. And that was when he heard the thunder that made him flinch.

Farmer’s gun.

&

The rest of the day was quiet, and Sally Downborough did not go to the farmwoods. She stayed with Tony Spot, inside the house, sitting on the living room rug, stroking his smooth coat. Food lay uneaten in his bowl—a first in this house, and they’d supposed it was the fox.

“Maybe it’s because they look the same,” Mrs. Farmer queried.

“Maybe,” her husband said.

“Maybe they were friends,” Sally supposed.

“Then he’s been a bad dog,” her father said.

No chickens were missing. They supposed that meant she hadn’t been creeping around for long. She’d been naught but skin and bone.

“Bad news,” Mrs. Farmer said at sundown, and when no one asked what, she continued. “Council’s talking about the farmwoods. Say Chickpool needs more space.”

“They’ll never cut ‘er down,” Farmer objected, “cause too much fuss.”

“There’s supposed to be a vote coming.”

“No one’ll agree to that.”

“I sure hope not, we already have to find a new caravan site.”

“That’s diff’rnt.”

“Where will I play if it happens?” Sally asked.

“It won’t happen.”

The day after, the sun was brighter, and the snow felt wetter. Sally took Tony Spot out for a walk. He didn’t seem keen on going to the woods, so they wandered the fields that were usually green. Rabbits sat on the hills, more noticeable than ever before. Some stood on their hind legs when spotting the dog, but he wasn’t interested in chasing them. What would happen if he caught it? He worried that Sally would tell him to (“Go get’em Tonieee!”), to try and lighten his spirits. He would have to prove to her that he was not a bad dog, like Farmer said—but Sally said nothing.

Tony Spot wondered what the fox had thought of him. He had wanted to be her friend. Tony Spot knew this because he had not wanted harm on her, and when he heard the gunshot he ran towards it with a worried whimper. Is that what a bad dog does? She was only hungry. But just as that thought was about to change him forever, he felt Sally’s gentle hand touch his ear. He looked up at her smile, and those eyes with more love than sight. And he moved in close to her as she knelt down beside him.

“Oh, Tony,” she said, “my Tony.”

Tomorrow, Tony Spot would go out sniffing for more. Things can always change, but the stories never end.

About Sebastian Day

Sebastian is a British third-culture author born and raised in Asia. Growing up, he lived in a remote Shan village on the Thai side of the Thai-Myanmar border where his parents worked as missionaries and aid workers in the region. After, he studied in Chiang Mai where he became acquainted with the international scene. His background in both rural and urban Thailand, along with the relationships he made in both places informed the writing of his debut short story collection, Gentian Violet.

Aside from Asian fiction, Sebastian also writes fiction about Britain, where he lived for a time after his studies and developed many opinions as a national raised abroad. Such works can mostly be found on his Substack, and in the Short Stories page of this website.

While storytelling is where his heart is, Sebastian Day also writes essays regarding a manner of different topics, and discusses books and his reading hobby on TikTok and Instagram.
